\begin{abstract}
The rapid transition from prompt-response generative models to autonomous, multi-agentic AI workflows introduces a critical human-factors vulnerability: the \textit{Delegation Paradox}. While end-to-end agent autonomy accelerates task throughput and lowers immediate subjective effort, removing interactive oversight friction induces severe vigilance decrement, cognitive offloading, and uncalibrated over-trust. In this paper, we formalize a dynamic cognitive-trust model for human-agent oversight and evaluate three workflow architectures—\textit{Low Autonomy} (step-by-step approval), \textit{High Autonomy} (zero-friction end-to-end synthesis), and \textit{Friction-by-Design} (risk-gated epistemic micro-friction)—within a simulated high-stakes financial auditing benchmark containing injected logical traps ($40\%$ error base rate). Across $N = 360$ simulated operator sessions ($7,200$ audit trials) spanning two behavioral profiles (\textit{Vigilant Verifiers} vs. \textit{Cognitive Offloaders}), two-way ANOVA demonstrates a highly significant main effect of autonomy architecture on Error Detection Rate ($F(2, 354) = 171.26, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.4918$). Zero-friction High Autonomy reduced error detection by $72.43\%$ relative to Low Autonomy ($11.46\%$ vs. $41.56\%$, Cohen's $d = 1.683$), inflating omission-based automation bias to $88.54\%$. Conversely, our proposed \textit{Friction-by-Design} framework restored Error Detection Rate to $49.58\%$ ($+332.73\%$ over High Autonomy, $d = 2.065$) while reducing simulated NASA-TLX workload by $19.70\%$ relative to Low Autonomy ($48.65$ vs. $60.58$, $d = 2.146$). We conclude with an open-source simulation suite and an IRB-ready human-subjects validation protocol.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern enterprise AI deployments are shifting rapidly from single-turn conversational assistants to multi-agentic systems capable of decomposing goals, querying external databases, reconciling financial ledgers, and executing multi-step actions with minimal human intervention \cite{park2023generative, wu2023autogen}. In domains such as financial auditing, clinical decision support, and software verification, multi-agent pipelines are frequently engineered around \textit{zero-friction} user experiences—presenting synthesized executive reports with single-click approval interfaces to maximize operational throughput.

However, decades of human-factors research in aviation and process control warn that increasing automation reliability and interface fluency paradoxically degrades human supervisory performance \cite{bainbridge1983ironies, parasuraman1997humans}. When autonomous agents emit fluent, high-confidence rationales, human operators experience \textit{cognitive offloading} \cite{risko2016cognitive} and \textit{automation bias}—the propensity to favor automated outputs over vigilant analytical verification, resulting in omission errors when subtle system failures occur \cite{parasuraman2010complacency, goddard2012automation}. In multi-agentic workflows, where upstream reasoning errors propagate across specialized sub-agents before reaching a human supervisor, uncalibrated trust creates systemic operational risk \cite{lee2004trust}.

Despite growing interest in human-AI teaming \cite{bansal2021does, bucinca2021trust}, three critical research gaps remain:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lack of Quantitative Multi-Agentic Oversight Models:} Existing automation bias studies focus primarily on single-step binary classifiers rather than sequential, multi-item agentic workflows where vigilance decays over time.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Interactions with Operator Profiles:} Little work quantifies how interface autonomy interacts with individual differences in operator reliance propensity (e.g., habitual cognitive offloaders versus vigilant verifiers).
    \item \textbf{Absence of Targeted Mitigation Mechanics:} Binary trade-offs between full manual review (high workload) and full autonomy (catastrophic error omission) lack principled intermediate architectures that inject friction selectively where epistemic risk is highest.
\end{enumerate}

To address these gaps, this study makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate a bounded-rationality mathematical model coupling Signal Detection Theory (SDT), dynamic trust inertia, and vigilance decay across sequential agentic oversight tasks.
    \item We execute a controlled $3 \times 2$ factorial computational simulation ($N = 360$ operator trajectories, $7,200$ trials) comparing \textit{Low Autonomy}, zero-friction \textit{High Autonomy}, and a novel \textit{Friction-by-Design} architecture across two operator cognitive profiles.
    \item We introduce the \textit{Cognitive-Safety Efficiency Score (CSES)} to jointly evaluate error detection, trust calibration accuracy, and cognitive workload.
    \item We provide a complete, reproducible Python simulation framework alongside a standardized human-subjects replication protocol to bridge computational modeling and empirical behavioral validation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Evolution of Automation Bias and Complacency}
Bainbridge's foundational analysis of the \textit{Ironies of Automation} \cite{bainbridge1983ironies} established that automating routine cognitive tasks leaves human operators with the harder supervisory burden of monitoring rare, complex anomalies under degraded situational awareness. Parasuraman and Riley \cite{parasuraman1997humans} formalized automation misuse, disuse, and abuse, while Parasuraman and Manzey \cite{parasuraman2010complacency} demonstrated that complacency and automation bias reflect a common attentional allocation failure driven by perceived system reliability. Goddard et al. \cite{goddard2012automation} systematically documented automation bias in clinical decision support, showing that false-positive or false-negative AI advice significantly impairs diagnostic accuracy even among domain experts.

\subsection{Trust Calibration and Cognitive Offloading in LLM Agents}
Lee and See \cite{lee2004trust} defined \textit{trust calibration} as the correspondence between an operator's trust and the automation's true capabilities, warning that over-trust leads to misuse while under-trust leads to disuse. In generative and agentic AI, large language models produce authoritative natural-language explanations that exacerbate calibration errors \cite{bansal2021does, pass2023overreliance}. Risko and Gilbert \cite{risko2016cognitive} characterized \textit{cognitive offloading} as the use of physical or computational external artifacts to reduce internal cognitive demand. Recent work on cognitive forcing functions \cite{bucinca2021trust} indicates that introducing deliberate interaction friction—such as delaying AI recommendations or requiring preliminary human judgment—can mitigate over-reliance, though naive global friction imposes unsustainable workload penalties.

\section{Theoretical Framework and Mitigation Architecture}
\subsection{Mathematical Model of Sequential Supervisory Vigilance}
Consider a human operator supervising a multi-agent workflow across a sequence of $M$ audit items $t \in \{1, \dots, M\}$. Let $E_t \in \{0, 1\}$ denote whether item $t$ contains a latent logical error ($E_t = 1$) or is valid ($E_t = 0$), and let $C_t \in [0, 1]$ denote the multi-agent system's reported confidence.

Each operator $i$ possesses baseline cognitive vigilance $V_{i,0} \in (0, 1)$ and trust inertia $\lambda_i \in (0, 1)$. Under autonomy condition $a \in \{\text{Low}, \text{High}, \text{FbD}\}$, effective supervisory vigilance $V_{i,t}(a)$ at step $t$ evolves according to:
\begin{equation}
V_{i,t}(a) = \text{clip}\Big( V_{i,0} \cdot \alpha_a \cdot \delta_a(t) + \beta_a \cdot \Phi_t - \gamma \lambda_i T_{i,t}, \; \epsilon_{\min}, \epsilon_{\max} \Big)
\label{eq:vigilance}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_a$ scales baseline attentional engagement by interface structure, $\delta_a(t) = 1 - \kappa_a (t - 1)$ captures sequential vigilance decay, $\Phi_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is an epistemic friction trigger, $\beta_a$ is the attentional restoration gain of friction, $\gamma$ weights trust-induced complacency, and $T_{i,t} \in [0, 1]$ is instantaneous operator trust.

Given error subtlety $s_t \in [0, 1]$, the probability of detecting an injected error ($E_t = 1$) is modeled via a bounded signal-detection response function:
\begin{equation}
P(\hat{E}_{i,t} = 1 \mid E_t = 1) = \text{clip}\big( V_{i,t}(a) \cdot (1.18 - 0.55 s_t), \; 0.03, 0.96 \big)
\label{eq:detection}
\end{equation}

Instantaneous trust $T_{i,t}$ updates asymmetrically following verification outcomes:
\begin{equation}
T_{i,t+1} = 
\begin{cases} 
\text{clip}(T_{i,t} - \eta_{-}, \; 0.15, 0.95), & \text{if } E_t = 1 \land \hat{E}_{i,t} = 1 \\
\text{clip}(T_{i,t} + \eta_{+} \lambda_i, \; 0.15, 0.98), & \text{if } E_t = 1 \land \hat{E}_{i,t} = 0 \\
\text{clip}(T_{i,t} + \eta_{0}, \; 0.15, 0.98), & \text{if } E_t = 0
\end{cases}
\label{eq:trust_update}
\end{equation}
where catching an agent error penalizes over-trust ($\eta_{-} = 0.14$), whereas missing an error reinforces uncalibrated trust inertia ($\eta_{+} = 0.04$). Trust Calibration Error ($\text{TCE}_i$) across $M$ items measures the mean absolute deviation between instantaneous trust $T_{i,t}$ and ground-truth item validity $1 - E_t$:
\begin{equation}
\text{TCE}_i = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^{M} \left| T_{i,t} - (1 - E_t) \right|
\label{eq:tce}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Proposed Friction-by-Design (FbD) Architecture}
Rather than forcing manual inspection of every low-risk item (\textit{Low Autonomy}) or allowing unverified batch approval (\textit{High Autonomy}), our \textbf{Friction-by-Design (FbD)} architecture dynamically gates epistemic micro-friction ($\Phi_t = 1$) using materiality and confidence-anomaly heuristics:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_t = \mathbb{I}\Big( \text{Materiality}_t > \tau_M \; \lor \; C_t > \tau_C \Big)
\label{eq:friction_gate}
\end{equation}
When $\Phi_t = 1$, the interface suppresses one-click batch approval and requires the operator to inspect a counterfactual failure prompt and verify a single supporting ledger cell before sign-off. Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the three oversight architectures.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\subsection{Benchmark Task and Controlled Error Injection}
We constructed a deterministic 20-item financial ledger auditing benchmark (`seed = 42`) spanning five accounting risk categories: \textit{Revenue Recognition Cutoff}, \textit{Duplicate Vendor Invoices}, \textit{FX Rate Misapplication}, \textit{Capitalized OpEx Anomalies}, and \textit{Tax Withholding Thresholds} (transaction values: $\$4,500$ to $\$148,000$). Eight items ($40\%$) contained injected logical traps with subtlety scores $s_t \in [0.55, 0.92]$. Crucially, the multi-agent pipeline reported high confidence ($C_t \in [0.86, 0.98]$) even on erroneous items to simulate realistic LLM hallucination overconfidence.

\subsection{Factorial Simulation Design and Provenance}
We executed a $3 \times 2$ between-subjects computational simulation ($n = 60$ simulated operator sessions per cell; $N = 360$ total sessions; $7,200$ total audit trials):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Factor A (Autonomy Architecture):} (1) \textit{Low Autonomy} ($\alpha = 1.02, \beta = 0.00$), requiring item-by-item approval; (2) \textit{High Autonomy} ($\alpha = 0.48, \beta = 0.00$), presenting a single batch summary with rapid decay ($\kappa = 0.012$); and (3) \textit{Friction-by-Design} ($\alpha = 0.88, \beta = 0.24$), activating micro-friction when transaction amount exceeds $\$45,000$ or $C_t > 0.91$.
    \item \textbf{Factor B (Operator Behavioral Profile):} (1) \textit{Vigilant Verifiers} ($V_{i,0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.76, 0.06)$, $\lambda_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0.28, 0.05)$) and (2) \textit{Cognitive Offloaders} ($V_{i,0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.54, 0.07)$, $\lambda_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0.56, 0.06)$).
\end{itemize}
\textit{Ethical & Methodological Transparency Note:} All $360$ trajectories represent synthetic cognitive agent simulations generated strictly to test theoretical bounds prior to live human recruitment; no human participant data are claimed.

\subsection{Dependent Metrics}
We evaluated six dependent variables: (1) \textbf{Error Detection Rate (EDR, \%)} of injected traps; (2) \textbf{Automation Bias Rate (ABR, \%)}, defined as omission error rate $100 - \text{EDR}$; (3) \textbf{Mean Response Time (RT, s)} per item; (4) \textbf{Simulated NASA-TLX Workload} ($0\text{--}100$ scale); (5) \textbf{Trust Calibration Error (TCE)}; and (6) the composite \textbf{Cognitive-Safety Efficiency Score (CSES)}:
\begin{equation}
\text{CSES}_i = 100 \times \left[ 0.50\left(\frac{\text{EDR}_i}{100}\right) + 0.30(1 - \text{TCE}_i) + 0.20\left(1 - \frac{\text{TLX}_i}{100}\right) \right]
\label{eq:cses}
\end{equation}

\section{Results}
\subsection{Main Effects and Interaction Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} summarizes descriptive statistics across autonomy architectures, and Table~\ref{tab:cell_results} breaks down performance across the $3 \times 2$ design. Two-way Type-II ANOVA revealed highly significant main effects of Autonomy Architecture across all primary metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Error Detection Rate:} $F(2, 354) = 171.26, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.4918$. Operator profile also exerted a strong main effect ($F(1, 354) = 117.64, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.2494$), alongside a significant interaction ($F(2, 354) = 7.41, p = .0007, \eta_p^2 = 0.0402$).
    \item \textbf{Mean Response Time:} $F(2, 354) = 899.93, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.8356$ for autonomy condition; $F(1, 354) = 626.96, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.6391$ for operator profile; and $F(2, 354) = 48.60, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.2154$ for their interaction.
    \item \textbf{Simulated NASA-TLX Workload:} $F(2, 354) = 884.38, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.8332$ for autonomy condition, with a significant crossover interaction ($F(2, 354) = 37.34, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.1742$).
    \item \textbf{Trust Calibration Error & CSES:} Autonomy condition significantly affected TCE ($F(2, 354) = 3.99, p = .0193, \eta_p^2 = 0.0221$) and overall CSES ($F(2, 354) = 105.31, p < .0001, \eta_p^2 = 0.3730$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pairwise Contrasts and Behavioral Mechanisms}
Welch's two-sample $t$-tests confirmed severe cognitive degradation under zero-friction High Autonomy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Omission Under High Autonomy:} EDR collapsed by $72.43\%$ from Low Autonomy ($41.56\%$) to High Autonomy ($11.46\%$; $t = 13.04, p = 3.00 \times 10^{-28}, d = 1.683$). Among Cognitive Offloaders, EDR fell to $6.67\%$ ($93.33\%$ automation bias rate).
    \item \textbf{Efficacy of Friction-by-Design:} FbD increased EDR by $332.73\%$ over High Autonomy ($49.58\%$ vs. $11.46\%$; $t = 16.00, p = 6.66 \times 10^{-37}, d = 2.065$) and outperformed Low Autonomy by $19.30\%$ ($t = 2.79, p = .0057, d = 0.360$) by preventing late-session vigilance decay.
    \item \textbf{Workload Optimization:} FbD reduced simulated NASA-TLX load by $19.70\%$ compared to Low Autonomy ($48.65$ vs. $60.58$; $t = 16.62, p = 1.09 \times 10^{-41}, d = 2.146$), yielding the highest overall CSES ($51.69$ vs. $45.26$ in Low Autonomy, $p = 2.19 \times 10^{-5}$).
    \item \textbf{Dwell-Time Mediation:} Across all $360$ sessions, mean review time correlated positively with EDR ($r = 0.6703, p = 2.67 \times 10^{-48}$), whereas TCE correlated negatively with EDR ($r = -0.2300, p = 1.04 \times 10^{-5}$), confirming that rubber-stamping ($<5\text{ s}$/item) mediates automation bias.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Human-Subjects Protocol}
\subsection{Resolving the Delegation Paradox}
Our findings demonstrate that the apparent efficiency of zero-friction agentic workflows is an illusion purchased through unmonitored tail risk. When multi-agent systems bundle complex ledger reconciliations into a single fluent summary, operators spend $62.5\%$ less time reviewing items ($4.99\text{ s}$ vs. $13.32\text{ s}$) and miss nearly nine out of ten injected logical errors. By gating epistemic micro-friction strictly to high-materiality or anomalously high-confidence outputs, \textit{Friction-by-Design} disrupts automatic System-1 rubber-stamping without re-imposing exhaustive manual review on routine transactions.

\subsection{Limitations and Empirical Validation Roadmap}
Because this study relies on computational cognitive modeling rather than live human participants, parameter magnitudes reflect theoretical distributions rather than empirical human variance. To enable immediate empirical validation without compromising ethical compliance, we developed a complete IRB-ready human-subjects protocol (included in our supplementary repository) specifying: (a) a counterbalanced within/between-subjects web interface (Streamlit/React) implementing the exact 20-item audit task, (b) informed consent and debriefing instruments, (c) behavioral telemetry capture (keystroke/cell inspection dwell time, override rates), and (d) post-task NASA-TLX and Jian Trust Scale questionnaires for $N \ge 90$ recruited accounting/engineering participants.

\section{Conclusion}
As multi-agentic AI workflows assume greater operational autonomy, human-AI interface design must transition from maximizing frictionless speed to optimizing calibrated vigilance. Our quantitative simulations show that zero-friction High Autonomy induces an $88.54\%$ automation bias omission rate, whereas risk-gated \textit{Friction-by-Design} elevates error detection to $49.58\%$ while cutting subjective workload by $19.70\%$ relative to manual item-by-item review. Incorporating targeted epistemic friction into multi-agent orchestration layers provides a practical, mathematically grounded safeguard for high-stakes human-AI teaming.
